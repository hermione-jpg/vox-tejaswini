## Overview
Parking is a series of small decisions, checks, and waiting moments-all happening while the driver is still moving.

VoxPark is a voice-first parking experience that lets drivers handle parking while they keep moving.


**Role** -     Product Designer, Conversation Designer, Builder  
**Duration** - 1 week  
**Tools** -    Figma, Next.js, React, TypeScript, ElevenLabs, Vercel, Cursor  
**Status** -   Working prototype, Concept validation

---

## The Experiment

I wanted to explore a simple idea:
## Can voice handle the parking interaction while the driver keeps moving?

The experiment explores how vehicle detection, parking availability, booking, payment, and navigation could work together through a voice-first interaction.

## The Problem

![news](/images/case%20studies/news1.png)
source: IndianExpress

![news](/images/case%20studies/news2.png)

Parking gets complicated at the entrance.

Finding availability, verifying a vehicle, making a booking, and waiting for the system to respond can turn a simple entry into a series of small interruptions.

But the articles only made me look closer at the problem.

### The Observation

I started looking at what happens during those interruptions.

The interesting problem wasn't parking itself. It was everything happening **while the driver is trying to enter**.

The driver is moving.

The camera is detecting.

The system is checking availability.

A booking may need to happen.

Payment may need to be completed.

The system is working in the background — but the driver is still expected to interact with it.

That became the opportunity I wanted to explore.

---

## Mapping the Experience

Before designing the conversation, I mapped everything that would need to work together to complete the parking experience -from the moment the vehicle enters to the moment it leaves.

![IA](/images/case%20studies/IA.png)

I started thinking about the experience through three layers:

**Driver**  
What does the driver need to do?

**System**  
What does the parking system need to know or update?

**Conversation**  
What actually needs to be spoken, and when?

This helped me move from thinking about a "voice assistant" to thinking about a **conversational parking system**.

The driver keeps moving while the system handles the work underneath.

## Designing the Conversation

Once the overall experience was mapped, I broke it into conversational flows.

The first flow focused on 
**Vehicle detection → Session → Availability → Spot selection → Booking → Navigation**

The second explored I explored three exit conditions rather than assuming every parking session ends the same way:
**prepaid ontime. prepaid overstay. postpaid**

![flow](/images/case%20studies/flow1.png)

![flow](/images/case%20studies/flow2.png)

The flows forced me to think beyond what the agent should say.
The conversation is only the visible layer. Underneath it is a set of tools that give the agent access to the real state of the parking session.

For every response, I had to ask:

**What does the system know right now?**

**What is happening in the background?**

**Does the driver actually need to respond?**

**What happens if something goes wrong?**

This is where the conversation started becoming a system rather than a script.

---

## Voice Is More Than Words

I soon realised that words weren't the only thing I was designing.

I was designing **how the words arrived**.

Speed, pause length, response length, acknowledgement timing, and pronunciation all changed how the agent felt.

![table](/images/case%20studies/table.png)

A shorter response could feel more natural.

A small pause could make the agent feel less immediate and robotic.

An acknowledgement could make a backend delay feel intentional instead of broken.

The voice itself became another interaction layer to design.

---

## Constraints

I deliberately kept the experiment within a few boundaries.

**Prototype scope**  
Real parking infrastructure isn't connected.

**Vehicle detection**  
Simulated rather than connected to a live ANPR system.

**Payment**  
Represented through the mobile interface rather than voice.

**Voice latency**  
Responses can't always be instantaneous.

These constraints helped keep the experiment focused on the **interaction model**, rather than trying to build an entire parking infrastructure system.

---

## Assumptions

To explore a realistic version of the experience, I assumed the eventual system could connect to:

- An ANPR/LPR system at the parking entrance
- A parking backend exposing live availability
- A QR-based mobile session
- Vehicle and user identification
- Reservation and payment services
- Parking sensors or cameras for occupancy

These weren't implemented as real infrastructure in the prototype. They defined the system the interaction was being designed around.

---

## What Broke

The prototype became more interesting once things stopped working perfectly.

### 01 — Voice quality

Some responses sounded too robotic or overly formal.

**I changed:**  
Response length, pacing, pauses, and phrasing.

---

### 02 — Pronunciation

Vehicle numbers and spoken time values didn't always sound natural.

**I changed:**  
How certain values were formatted and presented to the voice model.

---

### 03 — Latency

Tool/API responses created periods of silence.

Some flows also became too verbose while trying to explain what the system was doing.

**I changed:**  
Added short acknowledgement/progress feedback and removed information that didn't need to be spoken.

This was one of the more useful discoveries of the experiment:

> **Waiting isn't outside the conversation. Waiting is part of the conversation.**

## Making the Prototype Testable

I didn't want the demo to only work on the happy path.

Because real parking infrastructure wasn't connected, I added demo controls that let me manipulate the parking state and test different outcomes.

### Demo Controls

**New ticket / Reset session**  
Restarts the parking journey from entry.

**Vehicle type selector**  
Tests the flow with a car, bike, or randomly assigned vehicle.

**Lot full**

## What Could This Become?

The prototype currently simulates the parking infrastructure underneath the experience.

A real implementation could connect the conversational layer to existing parking systems:

**ANPR / LPR**  
Vehicle identification

↓

**Parking APIs**  
Live availability and reservations

↓

**Agent tools**  
Availability, booking, cancellation, session and payment actions

↓

**Payment infrastructure**  
Real-time checkout and overstay handling

↓

**Parking sensors / cameras**  
Spot-level occupancy and arrival verification

↓

**Navigation**  
Guidance to the assigned space

The opportunity isn't to replace the parking system with an AI agent.

It is to make the conversational layer **work with the systems that already know what is happening**.

---

## What I Took Away

### Voice isn't the interface. It's part of the system.

The experience only works when voice, UI, backend state, and physical infrastructure work together.

### Conversation starts with system logic.

Before deciding what the agent should say, I had to understand what the system knew, what it was doing, and what could happen next.

### Latency became a design material.

I couldn't eliminate every wait, so I learned to design what the user hears and sees while the system is working.

### The opportunity isn't "adding AI to parking."

It's removing unnecessary moments where the driver has to stop, look, tap, and wait — and letting the system handle more of the journey in the background.

> **I started by asking whether voice could make parking easier. I ended up exploring how voice can bridge the gap between a moving driver and a system that keeps working around them.**
